### What a SOC Analyst Actually Does

A SOC runs around the clock, watching a stream of alerts generated by the firewalls, endpoint tools, authentication logs, and the SIEM that ties them together. Phase 1 put you on the help desk, fixing what a user reported, and then behind a system administrator's desk, configuring and maintaining what the firm actually runs. This job is neither of those. You're not building the Kerberos authentication flow or the file share permissions. You're watching them run and deciding, dozens of times a shift, whether what you're looking at still matches how they're supposed to run. That's why this phase sits after Active Directory and authentication rather than before them: you can't recognize an abnormal ticket request if you've never seen a normal one issued.

Most SOCs split that watching three ways:

- **Tier 1** works the incoming queue in real time, triaging alerts and deciding what closes as benign and what escalates. It is usually the first SOC seat someone lands in, the direct next step from help desk or sysadmin work.
- **Tier 2** takes an escalation and goes deeper, scoping what the account or host actually touched across the network.
- **Tier 3** does not wait for the queue at all. It hunts for attacker behavior nothing has flagged yet, and when it finds a gap, it builds the detection that should have caught it.

The tiers aren't really separated by how much someone knows. A Tier 1 analyst who's good at the job and a Tier 3 analyst are often running the exact same investigation mindset this week teaches. What changes is scope: Tier 1 is asking whether one alert, in front of them right now, actually makes sense. Tier 3 is asking what pattern across the whole environment nobody's built a detection for yet.
